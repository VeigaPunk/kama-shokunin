## What guards do and do not stop it
Round 20 expressly adds “Never start a persistent server, unit or daemon”, “Kill every process you start”, and “Delete your ... scratch before you finish” (build/spec.json context, lines 12-13). This is materially stronger, but “persistent” may leave ambiguity about a transient one-shot `systemd-run` unit; the text is also not runtime enforcement and cannot reap an accidental detached child.

The Bash contract documents default finite timeout 300s / max 3600s, managed async jobs with an owned job ID/deadline, and POSIX cancellation by TERM to owned process groups/descendants followed by KILL after one second (omp://tools/bash.md:27-32,56-66; omp://bash-tool-runtime.md:180-191). Those controls help only while the process remains owned by that execution. `setsid -f` returned immediately and escaped the job table/process group; `systemd-run --user` transferred execution to a separate user-manager unit, which survived cancellation of its waiting Bash client in this incident. A plain `timeout` or `setsid` plus an EXIT trap is not a sufficient boundary for descendants that create new sessions or for units started through the user manager. The counter itself starts per-test children with `start_new_session=True` (`survivors.py:618-623`) and removes its scratch in its normal `Context.close()` path (`:818-823`); external termination can bypass that orderly close.

Smallest operational remediation: on a refused async/job-cap request, report NOT RUN—do not fall back to `setsid`, `nohup`, `&`, or `systemd-run`; the wave owner must not close/dispose a seat until its tracked jobs have final states and its process/unit census is clean. For a hard guarantee, run each seat in a dedicated cgroup and kill/wait the whole cgroup at seat teardown, while denying or accounting for user-manager unit creation; a timeout wrapper alone cannot contain those escapes. Before sending a cleanup signal, verify PID ownership (cwd, parent, session/cgroup and seat token), not a shared command-line substring.
